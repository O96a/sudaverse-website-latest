Tokenizing Sudanese Arabic: what a public benchmark shows

Four widely used tokenizers, 61 samples of Sudanese dialect text, and a simple question: how many pieces does each one cut a Sudanese word into? The answer is the case for a dialect-specific tokenizer.

  • Aamer MihaysiCo-Founder & AI Engineer

September 26, 20263 min read

On this page

  1. The benchmark
  2. Four tokenizers, four families
  3. Tokens per word
  4. Why a dialect-specific tokenizer
  5. Reproduce it

Every language model reads text as tokens, not words. Before a model sees a sentence, a tokenizer cuts it into pieces from a fixed vocabulary. When the vocabulary was built mostly from other languages, a word it has rarely seen gets cut into many small fragments. That costs more compute and a longer context for the same text, and it gives the model a less meaningful unit to learn from.

Sudanese Arabic is exactly the kind of text that falls through: a low-resource dialect, written informally, with its own vocabulary for food, family, places, music and everyday life. This artifact looks at how much that costs today, using the public Sudanese Dialect Tokenizer Benchmark.

The benchmark

The benchmark is a set of short Sudanese dialect texts, each a list of words or phrases on one theme, from greetings and proverbs to dishes, instruments and transport. The script sends every text through four tokenizers and counts the tokens.

The samples cover the vocabulary people actually use. Grouped by theme from their file names, they look like this:

Four tokenizers, four families

The four tokenizers come from different design traditions, which matters for how they treat Arabic.

Byte-level BPE works on raw bytes, so it never meets an unknown character, but an Arabic letter already takes two bytes in UTF-8, and a rare word falls apart into many merges. SentencePiece learns its pieces from raw text in the same way for every script. AraBERT’s WordPiece vocabulary was built for Arabic, and that shows.

Tokens per word

Dividing each tokenizer’s total token count by the 853 words gives its fertility: how many tokens it spends on an average word of Sudanese dialect.

The spread is wide. The two byte-level BPE tokenizers spend between 4.4 and 5 tokens on an average Sudanese word, more than three times what the Arabic-specific vocabulary needs. Across individual samples, GPT-4’s tokenizer used between 2.2 and 5 times as many tokens as AraBERT’s for the same text.

What this does and does not measure

Token counts measure efficiency, not quality. Fewer tokens is not automatically better: AraBERT is an encoder built for understanding tasks, not a generative model, and a vocabulary can also be too coarse. The benchmark is small, 61 short samples, and it counts tokens without special tokens. It shows the size of the gap, not a ranking of models.

Why a dialect-specific tokenizer

The gap is the motivation for Sudanizer, our byte-pair encoding tokenizer for Sudanese dialects, presented in Sudanizer: A Dialect-Specific Byte-Pair Encoding Tokenizer for NLP Arabic (IEEE FITAT 2026). The idea is simple: learn the vocabulary from Sudanese dialect text itself, so that distinctive words and expressions become whole tokens instead of fragments.

Building a dialect-specific BPE tokenizer follows a well-known recipe. What changes is the text it learns from, and how carefully that text is prepared.

Corpus preparation is where most of the effort goes. LLMCorpusKit cleans and repairs large Arabic corpora while keeping dialect vocabulary, and the Sudaverse Normalizer standardizes spelling without translating the dialect into Modern Standard Arabic. A tokenizer is only as good as the text it learned from.

Reproduce it

Everything above can be rerun from the public repository: the samples are in samples/, the dataset in dataset.json, and benchmark.py produces the counts. Word counts here split each sample on whitespace. If you run the benchmark on other tokenizers or other Sudanese text, we would like to hear what you find.
